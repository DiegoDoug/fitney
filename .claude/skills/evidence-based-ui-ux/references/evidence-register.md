# Evidence Register

Use this register to verify provenance and applicability before citing or operationalizing a research claim. The source collection is a discovery index, not an authoritative substitute for the papers.

## Applicability Check

Before transferring a finding, compare the source study with the present case across:

- participant population and expertise;
- product and interaction type;
- device, input method, and posture;
- physical and social environment;
- task duration, frequency, and interruption pattern;
- consequence of error or data loss;
- study method and measured outcome.

Label the transfer:

- **Direct:** closely matching users, tasks, setting, and outcome.
- **Adjacent:** meaningful similarities with explicit differences.
- **Analogical:** a useful concept from a materially different domain.

Direct evidence can still have methodological limits. Adjacent and analogical evidence can motivate a hypothesis or design direction, but cannot validate the present interface. State the differences that matter.

## Core Sources

### Scenario-based design

Carroll, J. M. (2000). *Five reasons for scenario-based design*. Interacting with Computers, 13(1), 43–60. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0953-5438(00)00023-0

- Contribution: explains how scenarios support reflection, flexibility, multiple perspectives, reuse, and stakeholder communication.
- Apply to: task modeling, concept design, tradeoff analysis, and design communication.
- Limit: conceptual design rationale, not a validated universal step-by-step method.

### Aesthetics and perceived usability

Tractinsky, N., Katz, A. S., & Ikar, D. (2000). *What is beautiful is usable*. Interacting with Computers, 13(2), 127–145. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0953-5438(00)00031-X

- Contribution: experimentally relates interface aesthetics to perceived usability in an ATM-like application.
- Apply to: distinguishing perceived from actual usability and treating visual quality as consequential.
- Limit: controlled application context; does not prove that beauty improves task performance.

van Schaik, P., & Ling, J. (2008). *Modelling user experience with web sites: Usability, hedonic value, beauty and goodness*. Interacting with Computers, 20(3), 419–432. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2008.03.001

- Contribution: connects pragmatic quality, hedonic quality, beauty, task performance, navigation, and mental effort.
- Apply to: multidimensional website evaluation.
- Limit: experimental website context; constructs must not be collapsed into a single vague “UX” score.

### Needs, affect, and user value

Hassenzahl, M., Diefenbach, S., & Göritz, A. (2010). *Needs, affect, and interactive products—Facets of user experience*. Interacting with Computers, 22(5), 353–362. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2010.04.002

- Contribution: links need fulfilment with positive affect and hedonic quality in reports of positive product experiences.
- Apply to: hypotheses about autonomy, competence, relatedness, stimulation, security, or meaning.
- Limit: reported positive experiences and attribution effects; needs are conditional design lenses, not mandatory features.

Park, J., Han, S. H., Kim, H. K., Cho, Y., & Park, W. (2013). *Developing elements of user experience for mobile phones and services: Survey, interview, and observation approaches*. Human Factors and Ergonomics in Manufacturing & Service Industries, 23(4), 279–293. https://doi.org/10.1002/hfm.20316

- Contribution: proposes usability, affect, and user value as UX elements using literature review, practitioner interviews, and user observation.
- Apply to: keeping functional, emotional, and value outcomes distinct.
- Limit: mobile-phone context and relatively small interview and observation samples.

### Heuristic evaluation

Hvannberg, E. T., Law, E. L.-C., & Lárusdóttir, M. K. (2007). *Heuristic evaluation: Comparing ways of finding and reporting usability problems*. Interacting with Computers, 19(2), 225–240. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2006.10.001

- Contribution: compares two heuristic sets and two reporting media within a structured evaluation framework.
- Apply to: structured problem reporting and cautious selection of heuristic systems.
- Limit: no significant effectiveness, efficiency, or inter-evaluator-reliability difference among the compared groups; do not claim one tested set won.

### Task coverage

Lindgaard, G., & Chattratichart, J. (2007). *Usability testing: What have we overlooked?* Proceedings of CHI 2007, 1415–1424. https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240839

- Contribution: analysis of nine commercial test teams found problem discovery related to task coverage and number of tasks rather than participant count in that dataset.
- Apply to: prioritizing representative task design before debating participant numbers.
- Limit: secondary analysis of one comparative study; does not show participant count is irrelevant in every study.

### Sample size

Schmettow, M. (2012). *Sample size in usability studies*. Communications of the ACM, 55(4), 64–70. https://doi.org/10.1145/2133806.2133824

- Contribution: critiques fixed sample-size rules and reframes estimation around variable problem visibility and study requirements.
- Apply to: rejecting automatic “five users” or “10 ± 2” prescriptions.
- Limit: sample decisions still require a study-specific rationale; the paper does not supply one universal replacement number.

### Perceived usability

Finstad, K. (2010). *The Usability Metric for User Experience*. Interacting with Computers, 22(5), 323–327. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2010.04.004

- Contribution: introduces the four-item UMUX and reports correlation and reliability alongside SUS.
- Apply to: compact measurement of perceived usability within a broader evaluation.
- Limit: subjective scale; do not infer actual task success from the score alone.

### Long-term UX

Kujala, S., Roto, V., Väänänen-Vainio-Mattila, K., Karapanos, E., & Sinnelä, A. (2011). *UX Curve: A method for evaluating long-term user experience*. Interacting with Computers, 23(5), 473–483. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2011.06.005

- Contribution: retrospective method for eliciting how and why experience changes over time.
- Apply to: learning, adoption, loyalty, and longitudinal experience questions.
- Limit: qualitative study with 20 mobile-phone users and retrospective recall.

### Engagement

O'Brien, H. L., & Toms, E. G. (2010). *The development and evaluation of a survey to measure user engagement*. Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology, 61(1), 50–69. https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.21229

- Contribution: develops a multidimensional engagement scale covering perceived usability, aesthetics, focused attention, felt involvement, novelty, and endurability.
- Apply to: products where engagement is an explicit outcome.
- Limit: validation centered on online shopping; engagement dimensions may behave differently in other domains.

### Feature excess and documentation

Blackler, A. L., Gomez, R., Popovic, V., & Thompson, M. H. (2016). *Life is too short to RTFM: How users relate to documentation and excess features in consumer products*. Interacting with Computers, 28(1), 27–46. https://doi.org/10.1093/iwc/iwu023

- Contribution: questionnaire and longitudinal evidence about limited manual reading, unused features, and negative experiences associated with over-featured products.
- Apply to: progressive disclosure, feature prioritization, and reducing routine dependence on manuals.
- Limit: consumer-product studies with demographic differences; documentation remains necessary in many complex or regulated contexts.

## Conditional Sources From the Collection

Load or research these only when their domain matches the request:

- Baxter & Sommerville, *Socio-technical systems: From design methods to systems engineering* — organizational and safety-relevant systems.
- Ji et al., *A framework for evaluating the usability of mobile phones based on a multi-level, hierarchical model of usability factors* — mobile-specific task, logical, physical, and graphical interface evaluation.
- The projective-techniques and sentence-completion study — exploratory research into feelings, needs, and values.
- The WebWOZ study — Wizard-of-Oz prototyping for speech, language, AI, and partially implemented interaction.
- van Deursen & van Dijk, *Modeling Traditional Literacy, Internet Skills and Internet Usage* — digital-skills and inclusion questions.
- The interactive audio-map study — nonvisual spatial navigation; do not generalize it into complete accessibility guidance.
- Lee & Yan, *The Potential of a Text-Based Interface as a Design Medium* — natural-language control in creative tools.

## Source-Boundary Warning

The Mauro Usability Science webpage contains peer-reviewed papers, informal articles, narrow domain studies, duplicates, obsolete redirects, and at least some mismatched link or abstract entries. Use it to discover sources, then verify bibliographic and substantive claims against the primary publication.

This register is not current accessibility, platform, legal, or safety guidance. Verify such requirements from authoritative current sources when they govern the task.

In particular, do not derive fixed touch-target, contrast, orientation, gesture, or assistive-technology requirements from the studies in this register. For Apple-platform work, consult the current Apple Human Interface Guidelines. For web accessibility requirements, consult the current normative WCAG material and its official supporting guidance. Record the version or access date when the requirement affects implementation or a compliance claim.
