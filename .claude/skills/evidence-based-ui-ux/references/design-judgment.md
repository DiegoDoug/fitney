# Product and Interaction Design Judgment

Read this reference when selecting between concepts, translating vague experience language into UX requirements, or deciding whether an experience is intentional rather than merely functional.

## Taste Is Contextual Judgment

UX taste is the ability to choose the interaction structure that best fits the product's users, tasks, risks, environment, and desired experience. It is not a house style, a trend detector, or permission to substitute preference for evidence.

A strong decision usually does four things at once:

- solves the important scenario;
- makes the interaction model legible;
- removes unearned complexity;
- gives the product a specific character through behavior, content, or pacing.

The last quality matters, but it never outranks task success, accessibility, safety, or user value.

## Write the Experience Read

Complete this statement before comparing concepts:

> For [user] doing [task] in [context], the experience should feel [qualities] so that [valuable outcome]. It should not feel [anti-qualities].

Use concrete qualities whose effect can be observed or tested. Prefer “fast to resume after interruption,” “calm under time pressure,” or “confident before committing money” over vague adjectives such as “modern,” “clean,” or “premium.”

If the user gives a vague adjective, translate it into possible behavioral implications and verify the intended meaning. For example, “premium” might mean confidence, discretion, service, craftsmanship, or exclusivity; those imply different flows and content.

## Compare Concepts Without Averaging Them

When the structure is genuinely open, create at least two concepts that make different tradeoffs. Keep them coherent enough to evaluate.

Compare each concept on:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Scenario fit | Does it support the important normal, interruption, error, and recovery paths? |
| Attention | Does it make the next important decision clear without hiding necessary context? |
| Learning | Does it use existing knowledge appropriately, and is any novelty worth learning? |
| Consequence | Does the amount of friction match the cost of error or commitment? |
| Adaptability | Does it remain coherent across roles, scales, devices, and states that matter? |
| Experience intent | Does it produce the intended confidence, pace, autonomy, or focus? |
| Product specificity | Is there a decision rooted in this product rather than a generic template? |

Do not total the dimensions into a fake precision score. Rank the options, name the decisive tradeoff, and state what evidence could reverse the decision.

## Control the Novelty Budget

Use familiar patterns where recognition reduces effort, error, or anxiety. Spend novelty where it creates a real product advantage: a better mental model, a faster expert workflow, a clearer comparison, a memorable moment, or a behavior uniquely suited to the domain.

Avoid novelty in several high-risk interaction areas at once. A new navigation model, unfamiliar control semantics, and unusual terminology compound learning cost even when each seems defensible alone.

## Practice Restraint

Remove an element, step, option, or explanation when its absence does not damage an important scenario. Do not remove necessary context merely to make a flow appear simple.

Good simplification moves complexity to the right place:

- a safer default instead of a repeated decision;
- progressive disclosure instead of simultaneous choice;
- recognition at the point of need instead of documentation;
- recovery instead of excessive prevention;
- a system rule instead of repeated manual work.

## Use Precise Critique

Avoid “clean,” “cluttered,” “intuitive,” “modern,” and “looks better” unless followed by the observable condition that makes the word meaningful.

Use this sequence:

1. **Observation:** what is present or happens;
2. **Effect:** what the user must perceive, remember, decide, or recover from;
3. **Principle:** the relevant scenario, constraint, convention, or evidence;
4. **Change:** the smallest coherent correction;
5. **Validation:** what outcome would show improvement.

Separate:

- personal preference;
- current trend;
- established convention;
- research or observed evidence;
- binding product or platform constraint.

## Prepare the Visual-Design Handoff

Do not prescribe colors, typefaces, radii, shadows, or aesthetic effects. Hand off:

- the Experience Read;
- three to five experience principles;
- explicit anti-qualities;
- the intended attention order;
- density and information-retention constraints;
- moments that should feel quiet, decisive, reassuring, efficient, or expressive;
- required content, states, and recovery behavior;
- conventions that must remain familiar;
- product-specific opportunities where visual distinction may be valuable.
